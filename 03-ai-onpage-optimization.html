Week 1 · Lesson 03 · AI On-Page SEO

A page that ranks, written to be read.

Search engines send the click; your page's only job is to earn the ranking and satisfy the person who arrives. In this lesson we dissect the anatomy of a page that ranks, match content to what searchers actually want, put AI to work drafting and optimizing copy without sliding into generic slop — and stack the E-E-A-T signals that tell Google a real human wrote this.

01 / 04

On-page SEO has exactly one job

On-page SEO is everything you control on the page itself — the words, the tags, the structure — that tells a search engine what the page is about and tells a human it's worth reading. Get it right and the same page satisfies both. Get it wrong and no amount of promotion saves it.

Beginners obsess over stuffing a keyword in as many times as possible. Google stopped rewarding that years ago. A page arriving in the results is really answering three blunt questions for the searcher: Is this about what I searched? Does it actually answer me? Can I trust who wrote it? A page that ranks answers all three — through its title, its headings and its body — and every on-page element exists to make one of those answers clearer.

The good news: pages that rank are boring in the best way. They follow a repeatable anatomy — a clear title tag, a single H1, a tidy URL, well-structured headings, helpful body content, internal links, described images, and schema. You are not inventing a page; you are assembling a known checklist. Explore each part below and note what AI can draft for you at every step.

Structure is a signal, not decoration

Notice what the mock page does not have: no keyword stuffed into every line, no ten competing headings, no meta description written for a robot. Each element sends exactly one clean signal. One page, one main topic, one H1. That clarity is what "optimized" actually looks like to a search engine — and it is far easier to build than the keyword-crammed version beginners reach for.

02 / 04

Match the content to what the searcher wants

Every search has an intent hiding behind it, and it usually falls into one of three types. Write the wrong type of page for a keyword and you'll never rank for it — Google already knows what kind of answer that query deserves.

An informational search wants to learn: "how to fix posture", "what is E-E-A-T". A commercial search is comparing before buying: "best posture corrector", "Frase vs Clearscope". A transactional search is ready to act: "buy posture corrector", "book SEO audit". Each intent needs a different page shape — a guide, a comparison, or a lean landing page. How to read the intent before you write

The fastest check: Google your keyword and look at what already ranks. If page one is full of guides, it's informational — write a guide. If it's comparison and "best of" lists, it's commercial — write an honest comparison. If it's product and booking pages, it's transactional — write a lean landing page. Google has already decided what that query deserves; your job is to match it better than the current results, not to argue with it.

03 / 04

Let AI draft and optimize — never mass-produce slop

Writing an optimized page used to take a full day of research and drafting. AI collapses that to minutes: outlines, first drafts, meta descriptions, FAQ sections, and coverage checks against the terms your competitors use. Your job shifts from writer to editor and fact-checker.

Here is what AI genuinely does well for on-page work: content briefs and outlines built from what already ranks, first drafts in a tone you choose, meta descriptions and title tags under the character limits, optimization passes where tools like Surfer, Frase or Clearscope flag the topics and questions your draft is missing, and FAQ sections drawn from the "People also ask" box. It is a genuine force multiplier — used with a human in the loop.

And here is the part hype merchants skip: AI will happily produce generic slop. Ask it for "a blog post about posture" and you'll get bland, sourceless filler that says nothing only your page could say — exactly the thin, mass-produced content Google's helpful-content stance is built to bury. The fix is specificity: a tight brief, your own first-hand experience, and a real human editing pass on every draft. AI drafts; you make it worth reading. The prompt decides everything — vague in, slop out. A tight brief and your own first-hand experience are what separate a page that ranks from the thin content Google's helpful-content stance filters away.

The editing pass that makes it human

Treat every AI draft as a talented intern's first attempt. Cut the throat-clearing intro ("In today's fast-paced world…"), replace generic claims with the one detail only you know from doing the thing, add a real example or a photo you took, and read it aloud once — if a sentence sounds like no person would say it, rewrite it. Ten minutes of editing on a two-minute draft is still far faster than a blank page — and it is the difference between content Google helps and content it hides.

04 / 04

E-E-A-T is a stack, and Google audits every page for it

Google judges content by E-E-A-T — Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness and Trust. No single tag sets it; it's the sum of many small signals that a real, qualified human stands behind the page. The more you stack, the more a page earns the benefit of the doubt.

Think about how you judge an article you've never seen before. You don't consciously score it — but a named author with credentials, first-hand photos, citations, a clear date, and a real "About" page each quietly nudge you from "who wrote this?" toward "I trust this". Google's raters do the same, and its systems approximate it. The fix is mechanical: stack the signals one by one until doubt has nowhere left to live. The stack, in the order to build it

Add a named author first — anonymous content struggles to earn trust. Weave in first-hand experience; a line like "when I tested this for three weeks" is something AI alone cannot honestly write. Then original photos or data you produced, cited sources for any claim you didn't verify yourself, an author bio with real credentials, a clear publish and update date, HTTPS and visible contact info, and an About page that sounds like real people. None of these is glamorous. Together they are the difference between content Google helps and content it quietly buries.

What to carry forward

You now know what on-page SEO is for. A page is not a keyword container — it is an answer built for one searcher and legible to one search engine. The anatomy is known, the intent is a deliberate match, AI writes the drafts, you make them human, and E-E-A-T is built signal by signal until doubt runs out of room.

  • Structure is a signal, not decoration — title, H1 and headings do the heavy lifting
  • Match the page to search intent — informational, commercial or transactional
  • AI drafts, humans make it worth reading — never publish generic slop
  • First-hand experience is the one thing AI alone can't fake — add yours
  • Stack all eight E-E-A-T signals so Google helps your page instead of hiding it
