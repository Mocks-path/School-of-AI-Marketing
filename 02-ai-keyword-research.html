Chase keywords with intent, not guesses.

Most "keyword hacks" sell a shortcut. This lesson sells a method: know what a good keyword actually looks like, read the search intent behind it, let AI brainstorm the topics — and accept that AI only suggests. The SERP and the market decide, and every volume number is an estimate.

01 / 04

What a good keyword actually looks like

Beginners chase the keyword with the biggest number. Professionals score keywords against criteria. The difference sounds small — it's the whole game.

A good keyword isn't a "high-traffic phrase." It's a keyword where six specific things line up at the same time. Miss one and the effort quietly wastes away: a term with 90,000 searches you can never rank for is dead. A keyword that ranks easily but nobody who searches it ever buys is dead. Here are the six tests, in plain language:

Test 1

Clear search intent

You can tell why someone types it — informational, commercial, transactional, or navigational. If you can't name the intent, you can't build the right page for it.

Test 2

Real search volume

Enough people actually search it to matter. Estimates are approximate, but a keyword with 20 searches a month rarely earns a whole page of effort.

Test 3

Winnable difficulty

Keyword difficulty is low enough that a newer site can realistically rank. Chasing a KD 80 term with a month-old blog is paying to lose.

Test 4

Business value

The searcher is someone you can actually help or sell to. "Best CRM for freelancers" beats "what is a database" if you sell CRM software.

Test 5

Intent match to your page

The page you'd build genuinely answers the query. If Google shows product pages and you'd publish a blog post, you've misread the intent.

Test 6

Beats the SERP test

Search it yourself. If page one is all giant brands and forums, ask what unique angle earns your spot — otherwise the SERP has already decided.

Score it, don't fall for the volume

Put those tests on a radar and every keyword becomes a shape. A good one is a big, balanced hexagon. A trap keyword is a spiky one — great on two axes, collapsed on one. 02 / 04

Every keyword carries an intent — match the journey stage

A searcher isn't a single type — they move along a journey: navigational, informational, commercial, transactional. Where a keyword sits on that spectrum decides what page wins.

Here's the trap of transactional intent ("buy running shoes online"): it converts hardest, so it's exactly what beginners chase first. But those terms are the most contested — every ecommerce giant bids on them, difficulty is highest, and the searcher has already decided where they might buy. You compete most fiercely for the searcher who is nearly gone.

Informational intent is the opposite ("how to break in running shoes"): huge volume, far less competition, and a searcher you can genuinely help before they're ready to buy. You earn trust early, then guide them toward commercial and transactional pages later. Drag the marker below and feel how the intent — and the right content — changes across the spectrum.

How do you read a keyword's intent?

You don't guess it — you read the clues. Informational: question words like "how", "what", "why", "guide", "tips" — the searcher wants to learn. Commercial: "best", "review", "vs", "top" — they're comparing before they buy. Transactional: "buy", "price", "discount", "near me" — they're ready to act. The fastest tell is the SERP itself: Google shows blog posts for informational, comparison pages for commercial, and product or store pages for transactional. In the next section, you'll see how AI turns hours of this classification into minutes.

03 / 04

AI brainstorms the topics. You make the call.

AI won't hand you a guaranteed ranking — nothing will. What it does brilliantly is compress research time: brainstorming, grouping, and drafting at a scale no human matches.

Manual keyword research means staring at a blank Keyword Planner, exporting messy CSVs, and grouping thousands of terms by hand. An AI assistant like ChatGPT or Claude does the brainstorming and clustering in minutes. What it can't do is verify — it doesn't know the true search volume, the real difficulty, or that the "great keyword" it invented has almost no searches. Treat AI as a fast, tireless research assistant whose numbers you always check against a real tool.

Brainstorm

ChatGPT & Claude

Ask AI to expand one seed keyword into 50 related terms, long-tail variations, and question phrases. Brilliant for ideas and topic clusters — but it guesses at volume, so never trust its numbers.

Estimate

Keyword Planner & Trends

Google Keyword Planner gives real (if bucketed) volume and CPC ranges; Google Trends shows whether interest is rising or seasonal. This is where AI's guesses meet actual data.

Measure

Ahrefs & Semrush

These give keyword difficulty, precise volume estimates, and who currently ranks. A difficulty score is a rank-chance signal — KD 15 is winnable, KD 70 is a fortress.

Confirm

Search Console

Google Search Console shows the queries you already rank for and their real impressions and clicks. The best keyword ideas often hide in terms you're on page two for right now.

The funnel: 50 → 10 → 3 → 1

Speed without discipline just means finding bad keywords faster. The professionals' habit is a funnel: brainstorm wide with 50 raw ideas (AI makes this cheap), screen them against the six tests down to a shortlist of 10, validate those hard with real volume and difficulty data down to 3, and build one focused pillar page or cluster around one. Falling for the biggest number at step one is how people skip the filters — and the filters are the method.

Be honest with yourself about the last stage: the one keyword you build around still might not rank. Google reveals the true winner over weeks, not on publish day. The funnel doesn't promise a top spot — it promises that when a keyword doesn't land, you wasted a research hour, not a month of content, because you validated with real data before you wrote a word.

Validate hard before you write a single word

Validation is a checklist, not a feeling. Run every shortlisted keyword through the same eight questions and let the count — not the tempting volume number — decide what happens next.

Each item below is binary on purpose. "Kind of matches the intent" is a no. "Difficulty is high but maybe I'll rank anyway" is a no. The discipline of answering honestly is exactly what separates research from wishful thinking. A quick prompt to kick it off: Act as an SEO strategist. What the thresholds mean — and the red flags

0–3: drop it and feel nothing — the funnel exists so that dropping is cheap. 4–5: the evidence is thin; refine the intent or pick a lower-difficulty long-tail before you write. 6–7: promising — this earns a real pillar page built around the cluster it anchors. 8: rare; publish, then track it in Search Console and improve as the SERP responds. Watch the red flags too: AI inventing keywords with near-zero real volume, chasing high difficulty because the volume looks big, a mismatch between the keyword's intent and the page you'd build, and trusting any single volume estimate as exact. Whatever the score says, the final judge is Google's own ranking — the checklist just decides what's worth writing.

What to carry forward

Keyword research is a filtering discipline, not a volume grab. Good keywords pass six tests at once, match the searcher's intent to the page you'd build, and survive a funnel that trims 50 raw ideas into one pillar page. AI compresses the brainstorming, grouping, and drafting — but the verification, and the judgment, stay yours.

  • Intent over volume · score keywords against criteria, never against the biggest number
  • Match the journey stage · informational earns trust before transactional earns the sale
  • AI brainstorms — ideas, clusters, questions · a real tool confirms volume and difficulty
  • 50 → 10 → 3 → 1 · funnel discipline builds pillars, not stray posts
  • AI suggests, the SERP decides · every volume number is an estimate — verify like it's true
